What is Module 3 about?

In Module 2, we studied words and word forms. In Module 3, we move one level higher and study how words form complete sentence structures. The main goal is to help the computer understand sentence organization, choose the best parse, and handle ambiguity.

Simple idea: words alone are not enough. The computer also needs to know which words belong together, which word depends on which, and which interpretation is the most likely.

Part 1

Theories of Parsing

How do computers understand sentence structure?

Summary figure for Part 1

Sentence→Words→Phrases→Relations→Structure
Main idea: Parsing is the process of analyzing how words are organized in a sentence. It helps the computer identify who did what, which words belong together, and how meaning is built from structure.
1

Parsing

Definition: Parsing finds the grammatical structure of a sentence.

Example: The smart student reads a book.

Why it matters: The system identifies the subject, the action, and the object.

2

Syntax

Definition: Syntax is the set of rules that controls how words combine into correct sentences.

Example: The boy eats an apple.

Why it matters: Correct word order helps the computer understand meaning.

3

Constituency

Definition: Constituency parsing groups words into phrases such as noun phrases and verb phrases.

Example: [The smart student] [reads] [a book]

Why it matters: Useful when we want to see phrase boundaries.

4

Dependency

Definition: Dependency parsing links words based on grammatical relationships.

Example: reads -> student (subject), reads -> book (object)

Why it matters: Useful when we want direct word-to-word relations.

5

CFG

Definition: A context-free grammar uses rules such as S -> NP VP to describe possible structures.

Example: S -> NP VP; NP -> Det N; VP -> V NP

Why it matters: A simple formal way to generate sentence structure.

Part 2

Parsing Algorithms

How does the computer build the parse?

Summary figure for Part 2

Grammar/Input→Search→Partial parses→Best structure
Main idea: Parsing algorithms are procedures that build sentence structure step by step. Different algorithms start from different places, but they all try to find a valid structure for the sentence.
1

Top-down Parsing

Definition: Starts from the sentence symbol and expands rules until it reaches words.

Example: S -> NP VP -> Det N VP ...

Why it matters: Easy to understand, but it may try useless rules.

2

Bottom-up Parsing

Definition: Starts from the words and combines them into larger units.

Example: The + student -> NP; reads + a book -> VP

Why it matters: Uses the actual input, but may build extra combinations.

3

Chart Parsing

Definition: Stores partial results so the system can reuse them later.

Example: If "the smart student" is found once, reuse it.

Why it matters: Reduces repeated work and improves efficiency.

4

Comparison

Definition: Different algorithms differ in starting point and search strategy.

Example: Top-down: sentence -> words; Bottom-up: words -> sentence

Why it matters: The goal in all cases is still the same: a good parse.

Part 3

Robust and Scalable Parsing

How can we handle messy text and large data?

Summary figure for Part 3

Noisy text→Cleaning / tolerance→Parsing→Useful output
Main idea: Real-world text is often noisy and large in quantity. Robust parsing tries to work even when the input is imperfect, while scalable parsing focuses on efficiency for large collections of text.
1

Noisy Text

Definition: Noisy text contains spelling errors, missing punctuation, informal words, or bad grammar.

Example: i luv this phone!!!

Why it matters: Common in social media, chats, and web documents.

2

Robust Parsing

Definition: Robust parsing still tries to extract useful structure even if the sentence is imperfect.

Example: student go school yesterday

Why it matters: The result may be incomplete, but still useful.

3

Scalable Parsing

Definition: Scalable parsing processes a large number of sentences efficiently.

Example: 10 reviews vs 10,000 reviews vs 10 million reviews

Why it matters: Important in search engines, social media analysis, and web mining.

4

Robustness vs Scalability

Definition: Robustness is about difficult input; scalability is about large volume.

Example: Robustness: bad text; Scalability: lots of text

Why it matters: Practical NLP systems usually need both.

Part 4

Hybrid Rule-Based and Probabilistic Parsing

Why combine rules with probabilities?

Summary figure for Part 4

Grammar rules→Candidate parses→Scores / probabilities→Best parse
Main idea: A hybrid parser combines explicit grammar rules with probabilistic evidence. This allows the system to generate possible parses using rules and then choose the most likely one using probabilities.
1

Rule-Based Parsing

Definition: Uses hand-written grammar rules.

Example: S -> NP VP

Why it matters: Easy to explain, but does not cover every real case.

2

Probabilistic Parsing

Definition: Scores candidate parses using probability.

Example: Parse A = 0.75, Parse B = 0.25

Why it matters: The computer chooses the highest-scoring parse.

3

Argmax Decision

Definition: Argmax selects the option with the highest score or probability.

Example: 0.75 > 0.25 -> choose Parse A

Why it matters: The value is maximum; the chosen option is argmax.

4

Hybrid Parsing

Definition: Rules propose structures; probabilities choose the best one.

Example: Grammar + scoring -> final parse

Why it matters: This gives more flexibility than only rules or only scores.

Part 5

Scope and Attachment Ambiguity Resolution

How does the computer choose the intended interpretation?

Summary figure for Part 5

One sentence→Many interpretations→Context / meaning→Chosen interpretation
Main idea: Sometimes a sentence is grammatically valid but has more than one interpretation. Ambiguity resolution means using structure, context, and meaning to choose the most appropriate interpretation.
1

Ambiguity

Definition: Ambiguity means there is more than one possible meaning or structure.

Example: The sentence is correct, but it may be understood in two ways.

Why it matters: The computer must decide which interpretation is intended.

2

Attachment Ambiguity

Definition: A phrase can attach to different parts of the sentence.

Example: I saw the man with a telescope.

Why it matters: Did I use the telescope, or did the man have it?

3

Scope Ambiguity

Definition: Words like every, all, or not can create different interpretations.

Example: Every student read a book.

Why it matters: One book for all? Or a different book for each student?

4

Resolution

Definition: Grammar, context, semantics, and probability help the system choose.

Example: Previous sentence context may make one meaning more likely.

Why it matters: Real NLP systems combine several clues.
